From what I know of MIT, there isn't one factor (especially not academic) that they look at to determine if you'll get in. You can have great grades, or do a bunch of extracurriculars, or have a ton of achievements, but you still wont be guaranteed to get in. In fact, those might be considered kind of a lower bound for getting in.
MIT says that they look for kids with an acceptable level of knowledge and ability (which you almost certainty meet given that you're in WSS), and beyond that just look for good, motivated people with some story. I talked to an MIT professor recently who said that they could triple the number of applicants they accept without any loss of academic quality. So when you're constructing your application, keep in mind that you want to seem not only highly capable, but also a kind and driven person.

Having a "story" is often recommended generally for college applications, and since I've been doing activities relating to science communication for like 8 years, I made that a primary focus of my application. It will probably be different for you, but try to emphasize a single thing which shows:
- academic prowess (e.g. for me it was enough knowledge to teach science effectively as well as having achievements in science communication),
- being a good person (e.g. I wanted to share my love of science with others), and
- demonstrating persistence (e.g. I have been doing it for a long time, like 8 years, and I said that I was interested in becoming a professor or teacher after I graduate MIT)
It was mostly dumb luck that my passion for science communication happened to line up so well with these three things, so I leaned into this way of structuring my application. If you don't have something similar, don't sweat it.

There wasn't really anything unexpected during the application, but I also started it really early (like I had been looking at it and had it in my mind since the beginning of summer, and I started working on it in august to submit by Nov 1st for early action) so I had enough time to go through everything well.
I would say to pay attention to the word counts and number of things you can submit. For example, they only allow 4 activities, so you should be intentional about what you pick, what you might be able to group together, etc.

MIT has been my dream school for as long as I can remember, so I wanted to do everything possible to get in, so this may be more work than actually needed. On the other hand, I know a lot of people who applied to MIT not as their primary choice and didn't get in.

MIT doesn't use college app, and I would group their application into:
- Basic information
- Essays
- Activities
- Portfolios

I don't really have any advice for the application except to spend a lot of time on it.
I wrote dozens of drafts and I explored multiple topics for every single essay, I spent a lot of time trying to organize my activities, and I did a maker portfolio, a research portfolio, and two music portfolios.
Get other people to look at everything as well: my mom helped me to revise a lot of my essays, I met with my guidance counselor for an hour or more every week to go over essays and applications, and I had a family friend who works in college admissions read over my essays a little bit before I submitted. I was really lucky with the amount of help I got and the people willing to look over it.

For my application (though it may change), the essays were about 
- Activities for fun
- Contributing to community
- Educational journey
- Field of study
- Learning from challenges

For pricing,
MIT offers very generous financial aid. If your family makes <200k per year and don't have a significant amount of assets, then you won't have to pay any tuition. If you're under 100k, I think you don't pay anything. Otherwise, its about 65k per year for tuition and ~80k per year total.
